\section{The Security Proof}\label{sec:proof}
\Cref{thm:main} is an instance of a theorem about any interrupt policy
(\cref{thm:policy}). Most steps of the proof are routine, and the two
that are not, \cref{lem:state-cell,lem:slot}, take up the rest of the section.

\emph{Proof outline.} The proof unfolds \m{model} and
applies \cref{thm:composition} one constructor at a time, from the outermost inwards,
with \cref{thm:sta-pred} for the state cell. The term is the listing of
\cref{sec:parameters}, with \code{par slot_5 (... slot_0)} as the pool, where
\begin{lstlisting}
slot_n :=
 map gamma_n id
  (swi b_n
   (maybe (map id (fun o => (true, o)) p_n)))
\end{lstlisting}
Here \code{gamma_n i} is \code{(slot_pid i.1 == n, f_proj i.2 n)}, \m{slot_pid} gives
the slot a pid names, and \code{p_n} is the process of slot $n$. The outermost constructor is a map, at the
equivalences of the theorem (\cref{fig:io}), with $p$ the rest of the term:
\[
\mathsf{map}\;\mathsf{inl}\;(\mathsf{inr\_or\_def}\;\mathsf{no\_output})\;p
  \in \NI(\Ein,\Eout)
\]
Inside it an event is a sum, at the equivalence $\Ein + \Eout$. No output
is hidden, so this never relates an interrupt to an output. So \code{inl} carries
$\Ein$ into the sum unchanged, \m{inr_or_def} strips the tag from outputs, and this
reduces to
\[
\mathsf{loop}\;p \in \NI(\Ein + \Eout,\, \Ein + \Eout)
\]
The loop asks the same of its argument, a map that drops the state and tags the output.
Writing $f$ and $g$ for \m{input_update} and \m{output_update}, and $\Ev$ for the state
equivalence (\cref{def:state}), this reduces to
\[
\mathsf{sta}\;f\;g\;\mathsf{init\_policy}\;p
  \in \NI(\Ein + \Eout,\, \Ev \times \Eout)
\]
This is the first hard step, \cref{lem:state-cell}. \Cref{thm:sta-pred}, with $P$ the slice invariant
$\Gated$ (\cref{def:gated}), needs $P$ to hold of \code{init_policy}, $f$ and $g$ to preserve $P$,
$f \in \NI_P(\Ein + \Eout,\Ev,\Ev) \cap \mathsf{PE}_P(\Ein + \Eout,\Ev)$ and
$g \in \NI_P(\Eout,\Ev,\Ev)$. It is $g$ that decides whether a secret handler runs
(\cref{sec:state-equiv,sec:update,sec:invariant}). What remains is the argument of the
\code{sta}, which receives the state with each event:
\[
\mathsf{map}\;\mathsf{pool\_input}\;\mathsf{id}\;(\mathsf{maybe}\;p)
  \in \NI(\Ev \times_R (\Ein + \Eout),\, \Eout)
\]
\m{pool_input} sends an interrupt to \code{None}, which \code{maybe} ignores and no level
sees, so a hidden interrupt stays hidden. It sends an output to the current pid and the
disk handler's output, related by $\Ev$ and $\Eout$. With
$\Eq \triangleq (\privvis + \pubeq) \times (\priveq + \one^\emptyset)$ on that pair, this
reduces to
\[
\mathsf{par}\;\mathsf{slot}_5\;(\dots\;\mathsf{slot}_0) \in \NI(\Eq,\Eout)
\]
The \code{par} asks the same of each slot, at its factor $\Eout_n$ of $\Eout$:
\[
\mathsf{slot}_n \in \NI(\Eq,\Eout_n)
\]
This is the second hard step, \cref{lem:slot}, which hides which secret handler runs. It
leaves $p_n \in \NI(\Ex_n,\Ey_n)$, for the input and
output equivalences of the process, where $\Eout_n$ has the form $\Ey_n + \one^S$.

\subsection{The state equivalence}\label{sec:state-equiv}

The proof's main choice is what the attacker may see of the state.

\begin{definition}[State equivalence]\label{def:state}
The state equivalence $\Ev$ (\m{state_type_equiv}) is
\begin{align*}
\Ev &\triangleq ((\privvis + \pubeq) \times \pubeq) \times \pubeq \times \pubeq \\
&\quad{} \times (\priveq \times \pubeq) \times (\priveq \times \pubeq) \times (\pubeq \times \pubeq),
\end{align*}
for the current pid, the previous pid, the reschedule flag and the slice counter, and
then the pending bit and mask of the NOP, disk and timer handlers, with $\times$
associating to the right otherwise. \Cref{fig:state} shades it.
\end{definition}

The conditions of \cref{thm:sta-pred} pull in opposite directions.
$\mathsf{PE}$ asks that an unobservable event leave the state related to itself, which is
easier to meet the more fields are private. $\NI$ asks that related events take related
states to related states, which is easier to meet the more fields are public, because
related states then agree on more of what the updates read. We make a field private only
where a condition forces it:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{The disk and NOP pending bits are private.} A disk interrupt is
  unobservable at $\Lo$ and sets the disk pending bit, so $\mathsf{PE}$ makes that bit
  private. The NOP pending bit is private by $g \in \NI_P(\Eout,\Ev,\Ev)$:
  when a slice opens, the disk handler takes the
  run the NOP handler was queued for, so during that run the NOP handler is pending
  in one state and not in the other.
\item \emph{The current pid is $\privvis + \pubeq$.} Its
  \code{inl} side holds exactly the two secret handlers (\cref{sec:global-state}),
  and which of them runs may differ between the two states, so the value inside
  \code{inl} is private. The $^\circ$ makes no \code{inl} unobservable, so
  the sum never relates an \code{inl} to an \code{inr}, and \emph{whether} a secret
  handler runs stays public.
\item \emph{Every other field is public:} the timer's pending bit, the masks, the
  slice counter, the reschedule flag and the previous pid. Nothing forces them private, and
  the next pid, whose tag must be public, is computed from them, by
  \m{initiate_next} directly and through \m{tick_slice} for the counter:
  two states that disagreed on a mask, or on whether a slice is
  open, could enter S in one and not in the other.
\end{itemize}
The shape of $\Ev$ makes most stages easy. At $\Lo$ two states are related exactly when
their public fields agree (\m{state_rel_bot}), and above $\Lo$ exactly when they are equal. So a stage
whose result has public fields computed from public fields alone, of the state and
of the output, is non-interfering; we call such a stage \emph{routine}.

The same conditions explain why \m{set_pending} sets one bit and stops: any decision
taken on an input step, such as starting a handler or moving the counter, would
change a public field, so everything an interrupt causes waits for a later output
step.

\subsection{The state update}\label{sec:update}

The conditions on $f$ hold on every state (\m{input_update_EP},
\m{input_update_NI}): the only unobservable events are disk interrupts, which set one
private bit (\m{set_pending_NI}), $\Ein + \Eout$ never relates an interrupt to an
output, and a fed-back output leaves the state alone. The hard obligation is
$g \in \NI_P(\Eout,\Ev,\Ev)$. On output $o$, $g$ is the
composite $\mathsf{initiate\_next}\;o \circ \mathsf{service}\;o$, where
$\mathsf{service}\;o$ is the service policy,
$\mathsf{advance\_policy} \circ \mathsf{observe\_policy}\;o$, and the
proof takes its stages one at a time (\m{policy_output_NI_on}, \cref{fig:lane}).

The service policy must be non-interfering on every pair of related states, which is an
obligation on the policy (\cref{thm:policy}). For the sliced policy this is routine
(\cref{sec:state-equiv}): both its stages read only the
counter and the timer handler's output, which are public. Each stage is
non-interfering on every pair of related states, so \m{fv_NI_comp} composes them
(\m{sliced_service_NI}, \m{unmasked_service_NI}); \m{initiate_next} is not, so
$g$ needs the invariant.
\m{initiate_next} is the same for every policy, and it reads the private pending bits. Its
first step, the scheduler's choice, is routine. Then the timer's
readiness is public, so both states agree on it. Otherwise, in each state the
secret handler ready to start is the disk handler, the NOP handler, or none. Disk in
one state and NOP in the other is harmless: both start an \code{inl} pid, whose
value is private. A secret handler in one state and none in the other is fatal: one
state enters S and the other continues a user process. So the stage needs
one fact of each of the two states it receives: unless all masks are set, a slice is
open exactly when a secret handler is ready (\cref{def:ready}).

Whether a slice is open is public. So two related states with this fact agree on
whether a secret handler is ready, and
\m{initiate_next} takes them to related states (\m{initiate_next_NI_on}).
The ordinary policy fails here and in the service policy.
\m{observe_handler_output} unmasks when any handler completes, the disk
handler included, so it writes public masks from a private output. And
\m{initiate_next} meets the fatal case: a bold arrow of \cref{fig:policy-ordinary}
taken in one run only, which is the counterexample of \cref{thm:leak}.

\subsection{The slice invariant}\label{sec:invariant}

In the states the sliced policy reaches, the fact of \cref{sec:update} holds whenever
\m{initiate_next} chooses: when not all masks are set, a secret handler is ready if a
slice is open, and neither is ready otherwise. This is an inductive property, which unreachable
states may violate. Two unreachable states outside a slice with the disk and NOP handlers unmasked, the disk
handler pending in one only, are related, and \m{initiate_next} starts a secret
handler in one and not in the other. So \comp{it:sta}, which asks for
$g \in \NI(\Eout,\Ev,\Ev)$ on every pair of related states, fails, and $\Ev$ cannot be restricted instead, because a characterised
equivalence relates every value to itself. So we use \cref{thm:sta-pred}, with $P$ the
invariant the sliced policy maintains between steps.

\begin{definition}[Slice invariant, \m{secret_gated},\newline \m{secret_gated_at_boundary}]\label{def:gated}
For a state $v$, write $\mathsf{masked}\,v$ when all three handlers are masked,
$\mathsf{masked}_S\,v$ when the secret handlers $S = \{D,N\}$ are,
$\mathsf{ready}_h\,v$ when handler $h$ is pending and unmasked, and
$\mathsf{open}\,v$ when the counter is not \code{0}. Then
\begin{align*}
\Gated\,v \triangleq{}& (\mathsf{open}\,v \Rightarrow \mathsf{masked}\,v) \\
&\wedge (\neg\mathsf{open}\,v \Rightarrow \mathsf{masked}_S\,v), \\
\GatedB\,v \triangleq{}& (\mathsf{open}\,v \Rightarrow \mathsf{masked}\,v \vee
  \mathsf{ready}_D\,v \vee \mathsf{ready}_N\,v) \\
&\wedge (\neg\mathsf{open}\,v \Rightarrow \mathsf{masked}_S\,v).
\end{align*}
\end{definition}

Between steps, then, the secret handlers are masked outside a slice, and a
handler is running inside one. Where \m{initiate_next} chooses, a slice may also be at
a handler boundary, where a secret handler must be ready: otherwise control would
return to user space with the slice still open. This disjunct, which follows the
\code{if} of \m{tick_slice}, is what keeps the slice going. It is the requirement on a
policy, and the NOP handler is how ours meets it: \m{queue_secret_run}
makes $\mathsf{ready}_N$ hold at every boundary. $\GatedB$ gives \m{initiate_next} its
fact:

\begin{definition}[Open iff ready, \m{open_iff_ready}]\label{def:ready}
\begin{align*}
\RIO\,v \triangleq{}& \neg\mathsf{masked}\,v \Rightarrow \\
&(\mathsf{open}\,v \iff \mathsf{ready}_D\,v \vee \mathsf{ready}_N\,v).
\end{align*}
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}[Ready at a choice,\newline \m{secret_gated_at_boundary_ready}]\label{lem:ready}
If $\GatedB\,v$ then $\RIO\,v$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Suppose not all masks are set. If a slice is open then a secret handler is ready.
If none is open then the disk and NOP masks are set, so neither secret
handler is ready.
\end{proof}

The invariant says nothing about the timer or disk masks at a boundary.
\m{queue_secret_run} masks the timer so that its handler cannot run inside a slice and
extend it, and unmasks the disk so that it can claim a run; the proof uses neither. With
the timer left unmasked the policy is also secure (\m{model_timer_unmasked_NI}), and a
timer handler run then extends the slice (\m{timer_unmasked_states_in_slice}).

The invariant passes through the output update as the triple
$\{\Gated\}\;\mathsf{service}\;o\;\{\GatedB\}$, an obligation on the policy, and then
$\{\GatedB\}\;\mathsf{initiate\_next}\;o\;\{\Gated\}$ (\cref{fig:lane}). The latter and
$\{\Gated\}\;\mathsf{set\_pending}\;i\;\{\Gated\}$ hold for every policy
(\m{initiate_next_gated}, \m{set_pending_gated}). The invariant is a predicate on the
model's states, and holds whatever the pool emits (\m{sliced_run_gated}, for the sliced
policy).

The rest of the proof uses three facts about the policy, so the result holds for any
policy that has them:

\begin{theorem}[Policy security, \m{model_policy_NI}]\label{thm:policy}
Under the hypotheses of \cref{thm:main} on the processes, if
\begin{enumerate}
\item\label{it:po-init} $\Gated\,\mathsf{init\_policy}$,
\item\label{it:po-inv} $\{\Gated\}\;\mathsf{service}\;o\;\{\GatedB\}$ for every output $o$, and
\item\label{it:po-ni} $\mathsf{service} \in \NI(\Eout,\Ev,\Ev)$,
\end{enumerate}
then \m{model} at this policy and these processes is in $\NI(\Ein,\Eout)$.
\end{theorem}

The theorem concerns security alone: a policy that keeps the secret handlers masked
forever meets all three obligations. The ordinary policy meets none of them: its initial
state leaves the secret handlers unmasked, and its service policy fails as
\cref{sec:update} showed.

The sliced policy meets all three, which gives \cref{thm:main}. \m{sliced_init} has the
counter at \code{0} and the secret handlers masked (\m{sliced_init_gated}). In
\m{sliced_service} (\m{sliced_service_gated}), \m{close_slice} sets
$\mathsf{masked}_S$, \m{queue_secret_run} makes $\mathsf{ready}_N$ hold, and every other
step leaves the masks alone. \Cref{sec:update} gave the third.

\begin{lemma}[State cell, \m{policy_state_cell_NI}]\label{lem:state-cell}
Under the obligations of \cref{thm:policy}, if
$p \in \NI(\Ev \times_R (\Ein + \Eout),\Eout)$, then
\[
\mathsf{sta}\;f\;g\;\mathsf{init\_policy}\;p
  \in \NI(\Ein + \Eout,\, \Ev \times \Eout).
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{thm:sta-pred}, with $P$ the invariant $\Gated$. $P$ holds initially
(obligation~\ref{it:po-init}), and
$f$ and $g$ preserve it by the triples above. The conditions on $f$ hold on every state. For
$g \in \NI_P(\Eout,\Ev,\Ev)$, the service policy takes two related states satisfying
$P$ to related states (obligation~\ref{it:po-ni}) satisfying $\GatedB$
(obligation~\ref{it:po-inv}), and so $\RIO$ by
\cref{lem:ready}, on which \m{initiate_next} is non-interfering (\m{policy_output_NI_on}).
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Slot, \m{slot_visible_NI}, \m{slot_hidden_NI}]\label{lem:slot}
If $p_n \in \NI(\Ex_n,\Ey_n)$, then $\mathsf{slot}_n \in \NI(\Eq,\Eout_n)$,
with $\Eout_n$ being $\Ey_n + \one^\Lo$ for a slot that no secret pid selects, and
$\priveq + \one^\Hi$ for the disk and NOP slots, whose $\Ey_n$ is $\priveq$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
A slot is a map around a switch. The proof chooses an equivalence $\Eb$ on its gate
bit, such that $\Ey_n + \one^{\Aw(\mathsf{true},\Eb)}$ is $\Eout_n$. The map
\[
\mathsf{map}\;\gamma_n\;\mathsf{id}\;p \in \NI(\Eq,\Eout_n)
\]
needs related pids to go to related bits,
$\gamma_n \in \NI(\Eq,\Eb \times_{LR} (\Ex_n + \one^\emptyset))$, and reduces to
the switch:
\[
\mathsf{swi}\;b_n\;p
  \in \NI(\Eb \times_{LR} (\Ex_n + \one^\emptyset),\Eout_n)
\]
This needs every observer to be aware of the gate or oblivious to the slot:
\[
\forall \ell.\; \ell \in \Aw(\mathsf{true},\Eb) \cup
  \Ob(\mathsf{swi}\;b_n\;p,\,\Eout_n)
\]
Awareness means a bit related to \code{true} is \code{true} and observable,
so the observer can trust the gate; obliviousness means it sees none of the slot's
outputs. Below the switch, the \code{maybe} and the map that tags each output reduce to
$p_n \in \NI(\Ex_n,\Ey_n)$, because \code{true} is related to itself.

At $\Lo$ the two
secret pids are related, and the slots fall into two groups (\cref{fig:equivs}).

\emph{Slots no secret pid selects show whether they ran.} Both secret pids map to \code{false}, so
related pids go to equal bits, and we take for $\Eb$ the equivalence $\Ef$
of \cref{fig:equivs} (\m{false_equiv}): $\priveq + \pubeq$ on a bit read as a sum,
\code{false} on the private side (\m{false_equiv_sum_rel}). So \code{false} is
unobservable at $\Lo$ and \code{true} is public. Every level is aware of
\code{true}, so
$\Aw(\mathsf{true},\Ef) = \{\Lo,\Hi\}$ and the slot's output equivalence is
$\Ey_n + \one^\Lo$: the attacker tells \code{Some} from
\code{None}, so it sees whether the slot ran. While a secret handler runs, all these
slots emit \code{None}; this is how the attacker sees that the system is in S. The
private user's slot is among them; its secrecy is carried by its private payload.

\emph{The disk and NOP slots hide which of them ran.} One secret pid maps to \code{true} and the
other to \code{false}, so $\gamma_n \in \NI$ needs \code{true} and \code{false} related at
$\Lo$: $\Eb$ is $\priveq$, and
$\Aw(\mathsf{true},\priveq) = \{\Hi\}$, so awareness fails at $\Lo$. The proof
uses obliviousness, and gets it by construction: both slots get a private payload
and the output equivalence $\priveq + \one^\Hi$, under which every output, \code{None} included,
is unobservable at $\Lo$. This is needed, because when a slice opens the disk slot
emits \code{Some o} in one execution and \code{None} in the other, and the NOP slot the
reverse.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:policy}]
Follow the outline from the outermost constructor inwards. Down to the state cell the
steps are routine, and \cref{lem:state-cell} handles the cell. Below it, \code{map},
\code{maybe} and \code{par} leave one obligation per slot, which \cref{lem:slot} reduces
to $p_n \in \NI(\Ex_n,\Ey_n)$. For the scheduler and the user processes these are
the hypotheses of the theorem. The handlers are non-interfering at their output
equivalences, public for the timer handler and private for the others, with the step
count classified like the output (\m{ir_handler_public_NI}, \m{ir_handler_private_NI}).
\end{proof}
