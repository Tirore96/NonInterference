\url{\repobase}, using Mathematical Components~2.5.0~\cite{mathcomp} with
Hierarchy Builder~1.10.1, deriving~0.2.2 and, for the coinductive definitions of

\paragraph{Figures} The runs drawn in \cref{fig:leak,fig:run-ordinary,fig:run-sliced}
are proved traces of the models, and the state rows of
\cref{fig:run-ordinary,fig:run-sliced} are computed by the model's own transition and
stated as lemmas (\m{immediate_states_with_dI}, \m{sliced_states_with_dI} and their
siblings), from which the figures are generated.

\paragraph{Types} Input and output types range over an inductive universe \m{Ty} of
codes, interpreted into \code{Set}, rather than over \code{Set} directly. This makes
reductions invertible, which the negative result needs: refuting non-interference
means showing that a trace is \emph{not} admitted.

\paragraph{Axioms} The development assumes nothing beyond the two lattice
parameters: no classical logic, and no uniqueness of identity
proofs. The latter is what dependent elimination on the type-indexed reduction
relations would need; we invert reductions through determinism of reduction
instead, one lemma per rule (\m{inv_mapO} and its siblings).

\paragraph{Levels} For presentation, this paper reads levels as the two-point
lattice. The mechanisation works over any partial order with a least
element, and so over any bounded lattice, as the calculus of Rafnsson et al.\ does,
for two reasons: it mechanises the existing theory as it
stands, and it lets other models, whose security claims need more levels, build on
it. Over such an order, a characterised equivalence must also be monotone downwards: what
a level relates stays related below it, and likewise for unobservability. Our models
need nothing more. Every equivalence in them treats the bottom level, read as $\Lo$,
apart from all others, so the proofs split on $\ell = \Lo$ against $\ell \neq \Lo$,
and the second case covers every other level at once; $\Hi$ in
$\priveq$ and $1^\Hi$ stands for all levels but $\Lo$.

\paragraph{Proof devices} A hook that only the proof needs, such as
\m{restore_invariant}, can hide behaviour. An earlier version of our model relied on
it to queue the NOP filler and to return control to the scheduler after a slice;
proving \cref{thm:noop} forced both into the interrupt policy, where they now are.

\subsection{Non-interference over finite traces}\label{sec:departures}

Our definitions follow Rafnsson et al., with one essential departure. The original
defines non-interference as a coinductive simulation over infinite streams of
labels, whose clauses apply at the head of a stream. We quantify over finite traces
instead, with closure clauses that apply at an arbitrary position (Definition 1).
This makes every proof in the development an induction on a trace.

The original definition is written out in \mechfile{theories/adequacy.v}{adequacy.v},
and \m{NI_paper} proves the two equivalent. The proof passes through infinite
streams (\m{NI_SNI}) and rests on two properties: finite and infinite behaviour
determine each other, which needs reduction to be deterministic (to assemble
finite runs into one infinite run) and never to block (to continue a finite run);
and insertion commutes with taking prefixes. Obliviousness, the other definition
that quantifies over behaviour, is likewise stated over traces and equivalent to
the original (\m{ObliviousDis_iff}). As a consequence, \cref{thm:leak,thm:main}
also hold with non-interference read as in the original, hypotheses included
(\m{model_immediate_not_NI_paper}, \m{model_sliced_NI_paper}).

One further difference is an alternative definition of characterised equivalence.
The original adjoins the distinguished value $\unobs$ to every type; we keep the
values as they are and make unobservability a predicate, tied to the equivalence
by requiring the unobservable values to form one of its classes.
\m{NI_lEquiv} shows that the two give the same notion of non-interference, and
this paper uses the original notation throughout.

The composition theorems themselves are proved directly for our definitions.

\subsection{Notation and the mechanised definitions}\label{sec:agreement}

The paper writes some equivalences differently from their mechanised definitions,
and keeps the two forms equal by proof. The mechanisation follows the original for
options: \code{Option} is a type code, classified by \m{eqmaybe}. Sums, which we
add, come there with two equivalences: \m{eqsum}, under which no sum is
unobservable, and \m{eqsum_L}, under which an \code{inl} is when its payload is.
The paper uses one sum former instead, $\eqo{X} + \eqo{Y}$ (\m{eqsum_LR}), with
$\doteq^\circ$ (\m{eqvisible}) and $1^S$ (\m{unit_equiv}). For each mechanised form,
an agreement lemma shows that, at every level, it relates and hides
exactly what its paper form does: $\mathsf{eqmaybe}(S,\doteq)$ is ${\doteq} + \one^S$
along the map from options to sums (\m{eqmaybe_sum_rel}, \m{eqmaybe_sum_dis}),
$\mathsf{eqsum}(\eqv{X},\eqv{Y})$ is ${\eqv{X}^\circ} + {\eqv{Y}^\circ}$
(\m{eqsum_sum_rel}), $\mathsf{eqsum\_L}(\eqv{X},\eqv{Y})$ is
$\eqo{X} + {\eqv{Y}^\circ}$ (\m{eqsum_L_sum_rel}), and $(\pubeq)^\circ$ is
$\pubeq$ (\m{eqvisible_public_rel}). No proof depends on the presentation, and
the calculus is the original's, with sums added.

Interrupts are the same technique applied the other way round: there the
mechanisation was changed to match the presentation. The input type is the sum
$\mathit{Unit} + \mathit{Unit}$, with the disk interrupt on the \code{inl} side, and
\m{in_equiv} is $\priveq + \pubeq$. The earlier definition, over a
two-constructor type, is kept as \m{in_equiv_interrupt}, and
\m{in_equiv_interrupt_rel} and \m{in_equiv_interrupt_dis} show that the two
agree.
