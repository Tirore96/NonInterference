\section{Background: Composable Timing-Sensitive Non-Interference}\label{sec:background}

This section recalls the calculus and definitions of Rafnsson et
al.~\cite{rafnsson2017}, in the form we mechanise. The one essential difference is
that non-interference is stated over finite traces; \cref{sec:departures} proves
it equivalent to the original.

\subsection{Processes, traces and time}

A process $p : \Proc{I}{O}$ has an input type $I$ and an output type $O$. It moves
in one of two ways, by receiving an input, $\stepI{p}{i}{p'}$, or by emitting an
output, $\stepO{p}{o}{p'}$, in any order. Processes are built from seven
constructors, whose reduction rules are given in \cref{fig:semantics}. The
operating system of \cref{sec:model} is a single closed term over them.

\code{out o} ignores its input and always emits \code{o}. \code{map f g p} translates
inputs by \code{f} on the way into \code{p} and outputs by \code{g} on the way out.
\code{sta f g v p} keeps a state cell \code{v}: an input \code{i} updates it to
\code{f i v}, and \code{p} receives the new state together with \code{i}; an output
\code{o} of \code{p} updates it to \code{g o v}, and the new state is emitted together
with \code{o}. \code{swi b p} switches \code{p} on and off. While the phase \code{b} is
\code{false} it emits \code{None} and \code{p} does not move; while it is \code{true} it
emits \code{Some o} for an output \code{(c,o)} of \code{p}. The phase is toggled by the
tag \code{c} and by the bit carried on each input. \code{par p1 p2} gives every input to
both parts and emits both outputs as a pair. \code{loop p} feeds each output of
\code{p} back to it as an input. \code{maybe p} makes the input optional: \code{None}
leaves \code{p} unchanged.

Reduction is deterministic, and a process never blocks.

Time is counted in output steps: every output step is one tick of a clock the
observer shares, and on every tick a process emits a value (in our models, a
component that did not run emits \code{None}). Input steps take no time. This makes
the setting timing-sensitive: a process that emits \code{o} one step late emits
something else, say \code{None}, at the original step, so a delay is observed as a
change of value.

\subsection{Characterised equivalences and non-interference}
Security is stated relative to a lattice of levels $\ell$. Everything in this paper
can be read with the two-point lattice $\Lo \sqsubseteq \Hi$: the attacker $\Lo$,
and $\Hi$, who sees everything (\cref{sec:generality} discusses arbitrary
lattices). Each input and output type carries a \emph{characterised equivalence}
(\m{cEquiv}) $\doteq$, which says, for each level $\ell$:
\begin{itemize}
\item $x \doteq_\ell y$: an observer at $\ell$ cannot tell $x$ from $y$;
\item $x \doteq_\ell \unobs$: an observer at $\ell$ does not see $x$ at all.
\end{itemize}
We keep the notation of Rafnsson et al.; in the mechanisation
$x \doteq_\ell \unobs$ is a predicate on $x$, which changes nothing
(\cref{sec:departures}). $\doteq_\ell$ is an equivalence, and the unobservable values form one
of its classes:
\[
x \doteq_\ell \unobs \;\Rightarrow\; \forall y.\;
  (y \doteq_\ell \unobs \Leftrightarrow x \doteq_\ell y).
\]
So at each level the type is partitioned into classes the observer cannot tell
apart, and one of them, the \emph{distinguished class}, holds the values the
observer does not see. It is empty when the level hides nothing. We write
$\eqv{I}$ and $\eqv{O}$ for the equivalences on inputs and outputs.

A trace $t$ is a finite word of input effects $?i$ and output effects $!o$. We
write $\epsilon$ for the empty trace and ``$.$'' for concatenation. A process $p$ \emph{performs} $t$ at
$\ell$, written $\perf{p}{t}{\ell}$, if it has a run with the effects of $t$, outputs
compared up to $\eqv{O}_\ell$ (\m{Trace}):
\begin{mathpar}
\inferrule{ }{\perf{p}{\epsilon}{\ell}}
\and
\inferrule{\stepI{p}{i}{p'} \\ \perf{p'}{t}{\ell}}{\perf{p}{?i.t}{\ell}}
\and
\inferrule{\stepO{p}{o'}{p'} \\ o' \eqv{O}_\ell o \\ \perf{p'}{t}{\ell}}
  {\perf{p}{!o.t}{\ell}}
\end{mathpar}

\begin{definition}[Non-interference, \m{NI}]
$p \in \NI(\eqv{I}, \eqv{O})$ iff for all $\ell$, $t$, $t'$, $i$ and $i'$:
\begin{align*}
i \eqv{I}_\ell i' &\;\Rightarrow\; \bigl(\perf{p}{t.?i.t'}{\ell} \Rightarrow
  \perf{p}{t.?i'.t'}{\ell}\bigr), \\
i \eqv{I}_\ell \unobs &\;\Rightarrow\; \bigl(\perf{p}{t.t'}{\ell} \Leftrightarrow
  \perf{p}{t.?i.t'}{\ell}\bigr).
\end{align*}
\end{definition}

The first clause (substitution) says that related inputs are interchangeable. The
second says that an input the observer does not see may be inserted into a trace
(insertion, $\Rightarrow$) or deleted from it (deletion, $\Leftarrow$) without the
observer noticing. The set of traces visible at $\ell$ is thus unchanged by anything
the observer at $\ell$ may not see.

\subsection{Equivalences}

Two equivalences do most of the work. Under \m{public_equiv} nothing is unobservable
and $x \doteq_\ell y$ iff $x = y$, at every level. Under \m{private_equiv} every value
is unobservable at $\Lo$, where all values are related, and the equivalence is
\m{public_equiv} at $\Hi$.

Composite equivalences are built from these. $\mathsf{eqpair}(\eqv{1},\eqv{2})$ (\m{eqpair}) relates
pairs componentwise, and no pair is unobservable. Its variants \m{eqpair_L},
\m{eqpair_R} and \m{eqpair_LR} make a pair unobservable when its left, its right, or
both components are. $\mathsf{eqsum}(\eqv{1},\eqv{2})$ (\m{eqsum}) relates a sum tag by tag and never
relates an \code{inl} to an \code{inr}, so the tag stays public even when the payload
is not; \m{eqsum_L} makes an \code{inl} unobservable exactly when its payload is.

Options are related by $\mathsf{eqmaybe}(L, \doteq)$ (\m{eqmaybe}), where $L$ is
the set of levels that can see \code{None}. It is the least equivalence with
\begin{align*}
\mathsf{Some}\;v \doteq_\ell \mathsf{Some}\;v' &\iff v \doteq_\ell v', \\
\mathsf{Some}\;v \doteq_\ell \unobs &\iff v \doteq_\ell \unobs, \\
\mathsf{None} \doteq_\ell \unobs &\iff \ell \notin L.
\end{align*}
So \code{None} is secret exactly for the levels outside $L$, and, by the
characterisation law, \code{Some v} and \code{None} are related at $\ell$ exactly when
both are unobservable there. The four instances we use are named after who sees
\code{None} (\cref{tab:eqmaybe}).

\subsection{Compositional theorems}\label{sec:composition}

The calculus comes with one non-interference theorem per constructor. Each derives
the equivalences of the composite from those of its parts, which is what gives the
equivalences of a large model their shape. Their side conditions are stated with the
following predicates on functions and levels:
\begin{align*}
f \in \NI(\eqv{I},\eqv{O}) &\iff \forall \ell, i, i'.\;
  i \eqv{I}_\ell i' \Rightarrow f\,i \eqv{O}_\ell f\,i' \\
f \in \mathsf{PU}(\eqv{I},\eqv{O}) &\iff \forall \ell, i.\;
  i \eqv{I}_\ell \unobs \Rightarrow f\,i \eqv{O}_\ell \unobs \\
f \in \NI(\eqv{I},\eqv{V},\eqv{V}) &\iff \forall \ell, i, i', v, v'.\;
  i \eqv{I}_\ell i' \wedge v \eqv{V}_\ell v' \\
  &\hphantom{{}\iff{}} \Rightarrow f\,i\,v \eqv{V}_\ell f\,i'\,v' \\
f \in \mathsf{PE}(\eqv{I},\eqv{V}) &\iff \forall \ell, i, v.\;
  i \eqv{I}_\ell \unobs \Rightarrow f\,i\,v \eqv{V}_\ell v \\
\ell \in \Aw(v,\doteq) &\iff \forall \hat v.\; v \doteq_\ell \hat v \Rightarrow v = \hat v \\
\ell \in \Ob(p,\doteq) &\iff \text{every output of $p$ at $\ell$ is}
  \doteq_\ell \unobs
\end{align*}
A function is non-interfering if it maps related values to related values
(\m{f_NI}, \m{fv_NI}), unobservable-preserving if it maps unobservable values to
unobservable ones (\m{f_PU}), and equivalence-preserving if an unobservable input
does not visibly move the state (\m{f_EP}). An observer is \emph{aware} of $v$
(\m{aware}) if it can tell $v$ from every other value, and \emph{oblivious} to $p$
(\m{oblivious}) if it sees none of $p$'s outputs.

\begin{theorem}[Composition~\cite{rafnsson2017}]\label{thm:composition}
For all processes, functions and equivalences of matching types:
\begin{enumerate}
\item (\m{out_NI}) $\mathsf{out}\;o \in \NI(\eqv{I},\eqv{O})$.
\item (\m{map_NI}) If $p \in \NI(\eqv{I'},\eqv{O})$,
  $f \in \NI(\eqv{I},\eqv{I'}) \cap \mathsf{PU}(\eqv{I},\eqv{I'})$ and
  $g \in \NI(\eqv{O},\eqv{O'})$, then
  $\mathsf{map}\;f\;g\;p \in \NI(\eqv{I},\eqv{O'})$.
\item (\m{sta_NI}) If $p \in \NI(\mathsf{eqpair\_R}(\eqv{V},\eqv{I}),\eqv{O})$,
  $f \in \NI(\eqv{I},\eqv{V},\eqv{V}) \cap \mathsf{PE}(\eqv{I},\eqv{V})$ and
  $g \in \NI(\eqv{O},\eqv{V},\eqv{V})$, then
  $\mathsf{sta}\;f\;g\;v\;p \in \NI(\eqv{I},\mathsf{eqpair}(\eqv{V},\eqv{O}))$.
\item (\m{swi_NI}) If $p \in \NI(\eqv{I},\mathsf{eqpair\_LR}(\eqv{B},\eqv{O}))$ and
  for every $\ell$,
  $\ell \in \Aw(\mathsf{true},\eqv{B}) \cup
    \Ob(\mathsf{swi}\;b\;p,\mathsf{eqmaybe\_aware}(\eqv{O},\eqv{B}))$, then
  $\mathsf{swi}\;b\;p \in
    \NI(\mathsf{eqpair\_LR}(\eqv{B},\eqv{I}),\mathsf{eqmaybe\_aware}(\eqv{O},\eqv{B}))$.
\item (\m{par_NI}) If $p_1 \in \NI(\eqv{I},\eqv{O_1})$ and
  $p_2 \in \NI(\eqv{I},\eqv{O_2})$, then
  $\mathsf{par}\;p_1\;p_2 \in \NI(\eqv{I},\mathsf{eqpair}(\eqv{O_1},\eqv{O_2}))$.
\item (\m{loop_NI}) If $p \in \NI(\eqv{I},\eqv{I})$, then
  $\mathsf{loop}\;p \in \NI(\eqv{I},\eqv{I})$.
\item (\m{maybe_NI}) If $p \in \NI(\eqv{I},\eqv{O})$, then
  $\mathsf{maybe}\;p \in \NI(\mathsf{eqmaybe\_nobody}(\eqv{I}),\eqv{O})$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Two of these carry substantial side conditions: the state update of \code{sta} must
be non-interfering and equivalence-preserving, and \code{swi} requires every observer
to be aware of the gate or oblivious to the gated process. These are the two
obligations the proof of \cref{sec:proof} has to discharge for the model.
